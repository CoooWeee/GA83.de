How to choose a Portuguese teacher

The right teacher starts with your goals.

Looking for the “best Portuguese teacher”? Start by defining what you need to do in the language. Use this guide to compare fit, ask useful questions and make a considered choice.

  1. Where will you use Portuguese?
  2. What would you like to be able to do?
  3. When can you practise consistently?
For adult learners · Beginner to returning learnerA decision guide, not a teacher ranking
01Define your fit

Choose an outcome you can picture.

“I want to learn Portuguese” is a starting point. “I want to introduce myself to my partner’s family and ask about their day” gives you and a teacher something specific to work towards.

Write one real situation, your current level and the time you can set aside. If there is a deadline, mention it as a planning constraint rather than expecting a guaranteed result.

For a trip

Prioritise the places you will visit, the questions you need to ask and the replies you need to understand. Bring a short itinerary.

For family conversations

Bring everyday topics: introductions, food, work and weekend plans. Ask how lessons will help you listen and respond.

For work

Describe the actual task: writing an email, joining a meeting or speaking with a customer. Use non-confidential examples.

For a fresh start

Ask for a clear starting sequence and a manageable routine. Tell the teacher what you have already tried and where you got stuck.

Match the variety to your context. If you plan to use Portuguese in Brazil, ask about Brazilian Portuguese. If your focus is Portugal or another Portuguese-speaking country, ask about the teacher’s experience with that context and the listening materials they use.

02Ask before booking

Six questions that reveal more than a slogan.

Use the same questions with each teacher you consider. Look for specific examples and clear explanations, then decide which approach suits you.

1.

Have you taught people with goals like mine?

Relevant teaching experience can help you judge fit. Ask what the teacher would prioritise for your situation, rather than relying only on how long they have spoken the language.

“How would you structure the first few lessons for someone preparing to visit family in Brazil?”

2.

What does a typical lesson look like?

Ask how a lesson balances speaking, listening, reading and explanation. A sample activity is more informative than a general promise that lessons are engaging.

“Could you show me the kind of speaking task you would use at my level?”

3.

How will you give me feedback?

People have different preferences for correction. Explain whether frequent interruptions help you or make it hard to finish a thought, and ask how the teacher adapts.

“Will we revisit my common mistakes, and will I have notes to practise afterwards?”

4.

What should I do between lessons?

Be honest about your available time. Ask for a realistic example of practice you could do with the resources and devices you already have.

“If I have fifteen minutes on most days, what would you ask me to practise?”

5.

How will we review progress?

Agree on an observable task, such as ordering food or giving a short introduction. Revisit that task to see what you can do more independently and what still needs support.

“What task could we use to check my progress after the first block of lessons?”

6.

What are the practical terms?

Confirm the lesson length, total price and currency, payment process, cancellation policy, time zone and materials. If considering a group, ask about level, size and missed sessions.

“Could you send me the current lesson options and booking terms in writing?”

03Compare formats

Pick a format you can keep attending.

A format is useful when it fits both your learning needs and your week. Compare the actual offering: group size, teacher availability and lesson structure vary between providers.

Use these trade-offs as questions, not assumptions about a provider.
FormatWhy consider it?What to check
Private lessonsTime to work on your specific situations and questions.How the teacher adapts the plan, and whether the pace feels right.
Group lessonsOpportunities to practise with other learners.Level matching, group size and how speaking time is shared.
Online lessonsAccess from home and fewer travel arrangements.Time zones, audio quality, platform and how materials are shared.
In-person lessonsA dedicated place to meet and practise.Travel time, location, accessibility and available session times.

Private or group describes who attends. Online or in person describes where the lesson happens. You can combine the two choices.

04Reflect afterwards

After a first lesson, look for clarity.

Feeling challenged is normal when learning something new. Use this checklist to reflect on the teaching experience rather than expecting to understand everything immediately.

Unchecked items are useful discussion points. This is a reflection tool, not a numerical rating of teaching quality. Checkmarks are not saved by this page.
